MoMA Acquisition Patterns by Artist Gender, 1929–2026

This chart tracks one number, decade by decade: on average, how many years pass between an artwork being made and MoMA acquiring it. There is a separate line for male and female artists. If MoMA mainly acquired women's work later, to correct for overlooked artists, the female line would sit above the male one. Instead it sits below it in almost every decade. MoMA has generally acquired women's work sooner after it was made, not later.

... Women's average wait minus men's average wait, across all 142,505 works. A negative number means women waited less.

How "acquisition lag" and the "Gap" are calculated

Three steps, done for every one of the 142,505 artworks before we average anything. The sign of the Gap is chosen on purpose, so it answers our question directly.

  1. Per artwork: Lag = year acquired − year created.
  2. Split into groups, one per decade and gender combination (for example, "1980s, female" is one group, "1980s, male" is a separate one). Then average the Lag values inside each group.
  3. Gap = women's average minus men's average. Example from the 2020s: 33.4 minus 46.9 equals −13.5. A negative Gap means women's work was acquired sooner after it was made. This is the opposite of what we would expect if MoMA were slowly correcting for overlooked women. A positive Gap would mean the opposite: women's work sat around longer. The 1970s is the only decade where this happens, and even then the two averages are almost equal.

Worth knowing before you trust this

  • 142,505 of the 160,705 artworks in the cleaned dataset are used here, not all of them. A row is excluded if either the creation year or the acquisition date could not be determined (about 9,453 works), or if the artist's gender was recorded as something other than exactly "male" or "female" (about 9,967 works, including missing gender and about 120 works by non-binary, transgender, or gender-nonconforming artists, too few to analyze as their own group here).
  • Sample sizes are very unequal. Men outnumber women roughly 6 to 1 in this collection overall, and even more before 1960. The 1920s has zero attributed acquisitions by women, so that point is missing, not zero.
  • A small number of records, 93 out of 142,505, show an artwork acquired slightly before its listed creation year. This is almost always because the creation date is approximate ("circa"), not a data error. These records are included in the averages. Their effect on the results is very small.

Conclusion

Research question: Is MoMA's increasing representation of women artists driven by acquiring their work in real time, or by retroactively "correcting" the collection, i.e., acquiring older, previously-overlooked work by women decades after it was made?

Finding: The data points away from retroactive correction. Of the 160,705 artworks in the cleaned dataset, 142,505 have both a usable creation year, acquisition date, and a gender recorded as exactly "male" or "female." Within that group, women's work is acquired sooner after it is made than men's work, not later. The average gap is about 5.7 years (22.9 years for women vs. 28.6 years for men). This holds true in almost every decade from the 1930s to the 2020s. Only the 1970s is close to even. If MoMA were mainly catching up on overlooked women artists years after the fact, we would expect the opposite pattern, a longer wait for women, not a shorter one.

Nuance: Acquisition lag has grown for both men and women over time. In the 1920s and 1930s, MoMA acquired art within about 10 years of it being made. Today that gap is 30 to 50 years for both genders. The gender difference sits on top of this larger trend, it does not replace it.

Limitations: 18,200 artworks are excluded from this analysis, either because the creation year or acquisition date could not be determined, or because gender was missing or recorded as something other than exactly "male" or "female." Among the artworks that are included, men outnumber women roughly 6 to 1, and even more so before 1960. The 1920s has no acquisitions by women at all. A small number of records, 93 out of 142,505, show an artwork acquired slightly before its listed creation date, almost always because the creation date itself is approximate.

What this doesn't prove: This shows a pattern, not a cause. We don't know why the gap exists; it could reflect deliberate MoMA acquisition strategy, market dynamics (contemporary women artists may be more visible/collectible sooner than in past decades), differences in which works get gender-attributed in the dataset, or something else entirely. This analysis can't distinguish between those explanations.

Source: MoMA collection dataset (github.com/MuseumofModernArt/collection), Artworks.csv. Lag = acquisition year minus creation year, averaged within each decade of acquisition.
